Getting a Piece Out in Ludo: What Are the Chances?

Three chances to roll a six. What happens before the turn is over?

What is the chance of getting a piece out in Ludo? Derive the 40.96% opening-turn probability and verify it with a million Python simulations.

In Ludo, we need a six to bring a piece out. If we get three chances, what is the probability of getting a piece out in that turn?

Before calculating, we need to settle one rule. What happens if we roll three sixes in a row?

Here I am considering the version in which three consecutive sixes cancel the whole turn, including the move that brought the piece out. With this rule, getting a six is only part of the problem. We have to see whether the piece is still out when the turn ends.

Let us work through it.

The rules we will use

Suppose all our pieces are still in the starting area.

  • We get up to three attempts to roll the first six. If none is a six, the turn is over.
  • On getting a six, we bring a piece out and roll again.
  • A second consecutive six gives one more roll.
  • If the next roll is anything other than six, the turn ends and the piece remains out.
  • If we get three consecutive sixes, the whole turn is cancelled. No piece remains out.

We will use a fair die: each face has probability 1/61/6, and one roll does not affect the next. We also assume that we bring a piece out whenever we get the first six.

These rules may differ from the version you play. The answer below depends on them.

Look at these examples before going to the calculation:

Rolls in the turn What happens?
2,4,32,4,3 No six in three attempts. No piece comes out.
2,6,32,6,3 The six brings a piece out. The turn ends on 3, and the piece stays out.
2,4,6,6,32,4,6,6,3 The sixes give extra rolls. The turn ends on 3, and a piece stays out.
2,4,6,6,62,4,6,6,6 Three consecutive sixes cancel the whole turn. No piece remains out.

Notice that a turn can contain five rolls. The three-attempt limit is for getting the first six; extra rolls can follow it.

First, what is the chance of getting a six?

On one roll, five of the six faces are not six. So

P(no six on one roll)=56. P(\text{no six on one roll})=\frac56.

To get no six in all three attempts, this must happen three times:

P(no six in three attempts)=56×56×56=125216. P(\text{no six in three attempts}) =\frac56\times\frac56\times\frac56 =\frac{125}{216}.

Either we get a six within the three attempts, or we do not. Their probabilities add to 1. Therefore,

P(get the first six)=1−125216=91216. P(\text{get the first six}) =1-\frac{125}{216} =\frac{91}{216}.

This is about 42.13%.

Why not simply use 3×16=123\times\frac16=\frac12? Because the three possible positions of the first six do not each have probability 1/61/6. For the first six to appear on the second attempt, the first attempt must fail. For it to appear on the third, both earlier attempts must fail. The correct addition would be

16+56⋅16+56⋅56⋅16=91216. \frac16+\frac56\cdot\frac16 +\frac56\cdot\frac56\cdot\frac16 =\frac{91}{216}.

Both methods give the same answer.

Now, can that six be cancelled?

Yes. Suppose we have just rolled our first six and brought a piece out.

For the turn to be cancelled, the next two rolls must both be sixes. The chance of that is

16×16=136. \frac16\times\frac16=\frac1{36}.

So, after getting the first six, the chance that our piece stays out is

1−136=3536. 1-\frac1{36}=\frac{35}{36}.

This includes both acceptable endings: a non-six immediately after the first six, or one more six followed by a non-six.

It does not matter whether our first six came on attempt one, two, or three. The rolls after it have the same probabilities in every case.

We can now put the two parts together. We must get the first six and then avoid cancellation:

P(piece stays out)=91216×3536=31857776≈0.409594. \begin{aligned} P(\text{piece stays out}) &=\frac{91}{216}\times\frac{35}{36}\\[4pt] &=\frac{3185}{7776}\\[4pt] &\approx 0.409594. \end{aligned}

Note

The probability that a piece remains out at the end of the opening turn is

31857776≈40.96%. \boxed{\frac{3185}{7776}\approx40.96\%.}

Think of it as roughly 41 successful opening turns out of every 100, over a large number of turns. It does not mean that every group of 100 turns will contain exactly 41 successes.

The remaining probability is

1−31857776=45917776≈59.04%. 1-\frac{3185}{7776} =\frac{4591}{7776} \approx59.04\%.

So, under this rule, we are more likely to finish the opening turn with no piece out.

NoteIf you want to list all the cases

Write SS for a six and FF for any face other than six. Let P(S)=pP(S)=p and P(F)=q=1−pP(F)=q=1-p. For our fair die, p=1/6p=1/6 and q=5/6q=5/6.

There are six ways for a complete turn to end successfully:

Complete turn Probability
SFSF pqpq
SSFSSF p2qp^2q
FSFFSF pq2pq^2
FSSFFSSF p2q2p^2q^2
FFSFFFSF pq3pq^3
FFSSFFFSSF p2q3p^2q^3

For example, FFSSFFFSSF means two non-sixes, two sixes, and finally a non-six. Multiply the five roll probabilities to get q⋅q⋅p⋅p⋅q=p2q3q\cdot q\cdot p\cdot p\cdot q=p^2q^3.

Only one row can describe a given turn, so we add the probabilities:

P(piece stays out)=pq+p2q+pq2+p2q2+pq3+p2q3=pq(1+p)(1+q+q2). \begin{aligned} P(\text{piece stays out}) &=pq+p^2q+pq^2+p^2q^2+pq^3+p^2q^3\\ &=pq(1+p)(1+q+q^2). \end{aligned}

The same two-part reasoning we used above gives

P(piece stays out)=(1−q3)(1−p2). P(\text{piece stays out})=(1-q^3)(1-p^2).

These expressions agree because 1−q3=p(1+q+q2)1-q^3=p(1+q+q^2) and 1−p2=q(1+p)1-p^2=q(1+p).

The four unsuccessful endings are FFFFFF, SSSSSS, FSSSFSSS, and FFSSSFFSSS. Do not use “six successful patterns out of ten” to get 6/106/10: these ten patterns are not equally likely.

Checking with Python

We have the mathematical answer. Now let us try the same rules many times on a computer.

For each turn, the program rolls a simulated die until the rules say to stop. It records whether a piece stays out. After many turns, we calculate

experimental probability=successful turnstotal turns. \text{experimental probability} =\frac{\text{successful turns}}{\text{total turns}}.

For example, if 399 out of 1,000 turns succeed, the experimental probability is 399/1000=39.9%399/1000=39.9\%.

Here is the code. You can skip it and go straight to the results if you only want the probability explanation.

import random


def piece_out(roll):
    # Up to three attempts to obtain the first six.
    for attempt in range(3):
        if roll() == 6:
            break
    else:
        return False

    # One six has occurred. A non-six now ends a valid turn.
    for extra in range(2):
        if roll() != 6:
            return True
    return False  # Three consecutive sixes cancel the turn.


def experiment():
    rng = random.Random(20261030)
    theoretical = 3185 / 7776
    checkpoints = {1000, 10000, 100000, 1000000}
    successes = 0
    print("Turns,Successes,Experimental,Theoretical,Error_pp")
    for n in range(1, 1000001):
        successes += piece_out(lambda: rng.randint(1, 6))
        if n in checkpoints:
            estimate = successes / n
            error = 100 * (estimate - theoretical)
            print(f"{n},{successes},{estimate:.8f},"
                  f"{theoretical:.8f},{error:+.5f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    experiment()

Save the code as ludo_experiment.py and run python3 ludo_experiment.py. It uses only Python’s standard library.

The function piece_out handles one turn. It returns True when a piece stays out and False when it does not. Its first loop looks for the first six. If that fails in three attempts, the turn is over. Once a six appears, the second loop checks the extra rolls: a non-six ends successfully, while two more sixes cancel the turn.

What the experiment gave

Using the seed 20261030, I get the following counts. The seed fixes the sequence of simulated rolls so that the experiment can be repeated.

10,000 4,050 40.5000%
100,000 40,961 40.9610%
1,000,000 409,796 40.9796%

These are checkpoints in one run. The first 1,000 turns are included in the first 10,000, and so on.

After one million turns, the experimental answer is 40.9796%, compared with the calculated answer of 40.959362…%. The difference is about 0.02024 percentage points.

They are close, but we should not expect an exact match. The number of successful turns varies from one random experiment to another.

Also notice that the result at 100,000 turns is closer to the calculated value than the result at one million turns. There is no mistake here. More trials generally give a better estimate, but the estimate can move away from the exact value for a while. It need not improve at every checkpoint.

A check without randomness

There is one more useful check in the Python file.

The longest possible turn uses five rolls. Imagine writing down five die results in advance, even if the turn will finish before we use all of them. There are

65=7776 6^5=7776

possible five-roll lists, all equally likely for a fair die.

We can check every one. For a list such as 6,2,4,5,16,2,4,5,1, the turn ends at the second roll; the last three numbers are simply ignored.

Exactly 3,185 of these 7,776 lists leave a piece out. That gives

31857776, \frac{3185}{7776},

the same answer as before. This check covers every possible five-roll list; it does not depend on getting a lucky simulation result.

NotePython code for the exact check

Run this after defining piece_out:

from itertools import product

successes = 0
for sequence in product(range(1, 7), repeat=5):
    rolls = iter(sequence)
    successes += piece_out(lambda: next(rolls))

assert successes == 3185
print(f"Exact check: {successes} / {6**5}")

The downloadable script also checks all six successful and four unsuccessful endings listed earlier, including where the rolling should stop.

The distinction to keep in mind is simple: getting a six within three attempts has probability about 42.13%; finishing the turn with a piece still out has probability about 40.96%. The difference comes from the turns cancelled by three consecutive sixes.
